The Evolution of Laughter

What 75 years of broadcast television data tells us about our relationship with comedy, and laughter.

By Rachel Vice

If you’ve ever watched a sitcom, particularly one from the United States or United Kingdom, you’ve likely encountered a laugh track. A laugh track is recorded laughter that layers on top of scenes in sitcoms, meant to cue the viewer to laugh themselves.

The convention of using a laugh track began in the early 1950s, with the invention of the “Laff Box” by a broadcast engineer at CBS, Charles Douglass. It has changed form over the decades, with the use of pre-recorded (canned) laughter falling away to recording sitcoms in front of live studio audiences.

More recently, their use has declined, but not without a few defining eras along the way.

The Rise of Silence

The Laff Box was invented by a broadcast engineer at CBS, Charles Douglass, in the early 1950s.

After its invention, the use of canned laughter among popular broadcast television shows quickly ascended.

Not long after, it fell out of public favor, as exemplified by Groucho Marx’s public condemnation of their use in a 1967 interview in the San Mateo Times

Thus, a new era began. With multi-camera sitcoms at their height of popularity, laugh tracks in the form of live studio audiences dominated. For decades.

Recently, there has been a new era afoot. Comedies with no laughter added began their steep ascent in the past decade.

Canned laugh tracks peaked in 1964, and within 5 years, collapsed almost entirely.

Studio audiences dominated television for decades, peaking in 1994, then seemingly fell off a cliff in more recent years.

Comedies that have rejected the use of injected laughter altogether have surged since 2019.

What does it mean that we stopped laughing together?

Over the past 75 years, there have been three distinct changes in how comedy television shows leverage laughter. From cueing us with artificial laugh tracks to a turn to "authenticity" with the use of a live studio audience to the absence of these cues altogether.

And while it's hard to say for certain what is ultimately driving these trends, it feels worth stating that the way we watch television has fundamentally changed, and with it, our relationship to comedy has changed too.

Gone are the days of my parents' generation, where friends and family congregated around a television set to watch this week's new episode of a favorite household sitcom. Over time, this routine has given way to something more insular, and a little quieter, as we have shows at our fingertips to watch in solitude.

Methodology

The data for this story draws on the top 30 highest-rated U.S. television programs for each broadcast season from 1950 to 2025, as ranked by Nielsen Media Research and compiled on Wikipedia.

Each show was matched against The Movie Database (TMDB) data to identify genre. For the purposes of this study, television programs were filtered to comedies.

Each comedy show was then classified into one of three categories describing its use of audience laughter. Categories included a canned laugh track (laughter added in post-production with no audience present), a studio audience (live laughter recorded and mixed in during production), or no added laughter.
